## Understanding the problem

The CUI tool is used by marketing and operations teams to build and launch customer campaigns — defining discount structures, generating voucher codes, and managing benefits across multiple global markets. At that scale, the tool needs to be precise. It wasn't.

Talking to users across teams, a consistent picture emerged. Every campaign launch involved a significant amount of manual coordination that the tool should have handled — things like updating multiple campaigns at once, or reusing a discount structure that had already been configured elsewhere. Instead, teams were duplicating work, passing information through spreadsheets, and relying on colleagues who knew the system well enough to use it without making mistakes.

The mistakes were the most expensive part. The tool gave no guardrails during campaign setup, no way to check logic before going live, and no clear view of what discount rules would actually do once applied. Errors made it through to voucher generation — and some made it to customers. The cost of those errors, across markets, ran into the millions annually.

Underneath all of it was a data problem. Campaign performance, customer segment targeting, reporting — none of it lived in the tool. Teams maintained their own Google Sheets, each with a partial view, and cross-functional decisions were made on incomplete information. The tool had been built around the system's internal structure, not around what different roles actually needed to see and do.

## Turning research into a case for change

With a clear picture of where the pain was concentrated, the next step was making it legible to stakeholders who hadn't felt it directly. I built a design business case that translated what users described into financial terms — mapping the cost of setup errors, the operational hours lost to manual workarounds, and the campaign delays caused by a fragmented voucher process. The headline figure was €6M annually in avoidable errors. That gave the project the weight it needed to move forward.

Alongside mapping workflows of 6 individual user roles, I mapped the underlying objects — campaigns, benefits, rules, conditions — and traced how the relationships between them were invisible to the people configuring them. The spreadsheets weren't a habit; they were a coping mechanism. The workarounds weren't laziness; they were the only way to get reliable information out of a system that didn't surface it.

From there, the design direction became clear: make the logic visible, bring data into the tool, and structure workflows around what different roles actually need to do — not around what the database needs to store.

## Design decisions

Three strategies, translated into screens

The hypotheses shaped three distinct design strategies, each targeting a different layer of the problem. Together they form the vision — not a single redesign, but a coherent direction across the tool's core workflows.

<SectionLabel text="Strategy 01" />

## Campaign overview & detail page

The campaigns list was the first thing every user hit every day — but it gave almost no useful information at a glance. Status, summary metrics, and the benefit driving each campaign were all buried or absent. The detail page was similarly flat: it showed what a campaign was, not how it was performing or whether anything needed attention.

<ImagePair
  src1="/Work/CUI/CUI-Before-Campaigns-List.png"
  alt1="Original campaigns list — no metrics, no status, no filtering"
  src2="/Work/CUI/CUI-Before-Campaign-Detail.png"
  alt2="Original campaign detail — raw data fields, no performance context"
  caption="The original tool before the redesign. The list gave no useful information at a glance; the detail page showed data fields, not what users needed to act on."
/>

The redesign leads with a live activity summary — running, disabled, scheduled, expired — before the list. Campaign cards now include channel, partner, goal, and key metrics inline. Bulk editing lets users act on multiple campaigns at once. The benefit definition linked to any campaign is a single click away.

One of the more deliberate decisions was how filtering was designed. The query filters on the overview — status, redemption rate, benefit expiration date, market, channel, customer segment — use exactly the same parameters and terminology as the campaign creation flow. A user who just configured a campaign for a specific market with a redemption target can immediately filter the overview by those same values to monitor it. No translation step, no re-learning the vocabulary of a different screen. The system speaks one language throughout.

Filter presets extend this: teams can save the combinations they reach for regularly — active campaigns in Norway, benefits expiring within 30 days, campaigns above a 10% redemption rate — and return to them without rebuilding the query. This directly replaces the per-market tracking spreadsheets teams were maintaining outside the tool.

<FullImage src="/Work/CUI/Campaign Overview.png" alt="Campaign overview — live activity summary, filtering by status and benefit type, bulk actions" contained />

On the detail page, the bet was on consolidation. A campaign summary at the top gives a snapshot of performance and settings together. Customer segment, goal, and benefit definition are explicit sections — not tabs or buried fields. AI-driven recommendations surface when a campaign underperforms. A material preview connects to a QA check that flags conflicts before launch.

## Benefit definition overview

Benefit definitions were effectively invisible. Users knew a benefit existed somewhere in the system — but couldn't easily see which campaigns were using it, whether it was about to expire, or how its performance compared across markets. Managing them meant hunting across screens and cross-referencing spreadsheets.

<ImagePair
  src1="/Work/CUI/CUI-Before-Discount-Settings-List.png"
  alt1="Original discount settings list — no filtering, no usage visibility, no expiry warnings"
  src2="/Work/CUI/CUI-Before-Discount-Settings-Detail.png"
  alt2="Original discount settings detail — raw fields with no performance context or change history"
  caption="The original benefit/discount settings before the redesign. No way to see which campaigns were using a definition, or how it was performing."
/>

The overview applies the same filtering logic used in the creation flow. When setting up a benefit definition, users specify status, usage limit, expiration type, and customer segment. Those exact fields become the filter parameters on the overview — so the mental model built during creation transfers directly to how users search and monitor afterward. Status, expiration date, usage limit, and market filters all use the same vocabulary, the same values, the same structure. A user who created a benefit with a usage limit of 2 for new customers can filter the overview for exactly that configuration in seconds.

Bulk actions — deactivate, duplicate, approve — operate on the same selection, so teams can act on a cohort of benefits without switching contexts. Each definition shows which campaigns are using it directly in the list, replacing the separate lookup that was causing most of the confusion.

<FullImage src="/Work/CUI/Benefits definition overview.jpg" alt="Benefit definitions overview — filtering by status and expiration, usage visibility, expiry warnings inline" contained />

The detail page extends this further. A human-readable summary of what the benefit actually does serves as a built-in QA check before it's finalised. CSV upload support for SKU-based rules replaces the manual entry that was causing the most configuration errors. A change history and approval log makes modifications traceable. And cross-market comparison gives local and global teams a shared view of how the same benefit structure performs in different contexts — closing the loop on the data that was previously scattered across Google Sheets.

## Benefit definition creation flow

Creating a benefit definition was the most technically demanding part of the tool — and the least supported. Users had to understand how conditions and effects related to each other without any visual guidance, fill in fields whose purpose wasn't explained, and validate their own logic in a spreadsheet after the fact.

<ImagePair
  src1="/Work/CUI/CUI-Before-Create-Benefit-1.png"
  alt1="Original benefit creation — flat form with no guidance on how conditions and effects relate"
  src2="/Work/CUI/CUI-Before-Create-Benefit-2.png"
  alt2="Original benefit creation — SKU rules entered manually with no structure or validation"
  caption="The original benefit definition creation flow. No sidebar, no process visibility, no indication of what each field would actually do."
/>

The redesigned flow combines conditions and effects into a single unified view — so the relationship between "when this applies" and "what it does" is always visible together. Each step in the sidebar makes the process legible: create details, set schedule, define rules, QA review. Name and description fields are now part of the creation flow rather than an afterthought, so every definition is documented at the point of creation.

<FullImage src="/Work/CUI/CBD-04.jpg" alt="Benefit definition creation flow — conditions and effects in a single unified view with sidebar progress" contained />

Mixing and matching existing conditions with new effects — the most common task for experienced users — is now a first-class interaction rather than a workaround. Definitions can be saved as templates and reused across campaigns, directly addressing the duplication that was costing teams hours every week.

## What vision work is actually for

This project didn't ship a redesign. The output was a design business case and a high-fidelity vision — a clear picture of where the product should go, before anyone starts making incremental changes. Without that, every incremental change just adds to the confusion.

Honest about outcomes: there's no shipping data because nothing shipped. The value of this work is directional — it gave the product team a shared language for what a campaign and benefit actually are, a costed argument for why the problem matters, and a concrete vision to build toward incrementally.

The moment a UX problem reveals itself to be a systems problem is when the design work shifts from "how should this look?" to "how should this work?" That's where this project lived.
