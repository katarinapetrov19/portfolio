## A 10x ambition with a 2-week window

HelloFresh's most prioritised goal was scaling its global recipe offer from 50 recipes per week to 500. A 10x expansion. Not a product tweak — a fundamental change to how fast the entire Food Systems pipeline needed to operate. Seven separate internal tools handled different pieces of this process: recipe creation, content management, compliance checks, menu planning, demand forecasting, procurement. None of them talked to each other. No one had ever tried to unify them.

The ask that arrived was deliberately open: imagine what it could look like if HelloFresh could take a recipe from creation to the weekly menu in one week. Show us something real, fast. No flows, no existing UI, no handoff spec. Just the business need and a deadline.

<SectionLabel text="Foundation" />

## The research that made speed possible

Project Eleven didn't start cold. Before the sprint began, I had led a foundational research initiative mapping HelloFresh's Food Systems processes end-to-end — specifically in the context of migrating the US market to international tooling. That work covered 53 stakeholders across Tech and Ops, six distinct user groups, and 130 mapped steps across the full pipeline.

That prior research gave the sprint its backbone. The complexity of the pipeline — and the case for unifying it — wasn't hypothetical when the two weeks started. It was already documented. The fragmentation wasn't a discovery; it was a known fact with a mapped cost. That meant week one could skip the "understand the domain" phase entirely and move straight to defining what a unified system would actually need to do.

This is an underappreciated part of how fast work at this quality becomes possible: the speed wasn't reckless, it was earned by the investment that came before it.

## Structure first, then AI — in that order

The most important decision of the sprint was made before any tool was opened: spend week one defining, not designing. The pipeline was owned by different PMs across different parts of the business. Getting user goals, JTBDs, and data flows right required iteration with the full group — and that foundation had to be solid before anything else could move fast without thrashing.

Once the structure was there, AI compressed everything that followed. But it's worth being precise about what that means — because "I used AI" covers a lot of very different things. Here, it meant prompting from artifacts that already existed: JTBDs fed AI to generate feature sets; workflow maps fed AI to scaffold UI; Figma screenshots fed iterative refinement. The AI was downstream of thinking, not a replacement for it. It removed the translation layer between knowing what was needed and having something to react to.

**Week 1 — Define**

Whiteboard flow charts, a full-pipeline workflow map, JTBDs per user group, AI-generated feature set. Check-ins with all PMs to validate the structure before building anything.

**Week 2 — Design**

Magic Patterns and v0 prompting from the workflow map and predefined user actions. Figma screenshots as prompt inputs. Five prototype versions incorporating live feedback. No handoff — design and prototype happened simultaneously.

**After — Iterate**

C-level presentation, feedback in hours, hi-fi prototype in a single session. The cycle from leadership input to working artifact collapsed from weeks to hours. 372 prompts total.

## What vibe coding actually changed

Vibe coding — prompting AI tools directly into working UI — is often framed as a speed story. It is that. But the more interesting change is what becomes *possible*, not just faster. When the gap between a design decision and a testable artifact collapses from days to minutes, the nature of the work shifts. You stop designing in the abstract and start reacting to real things. Iteration becomes the primary mode rather than a final phase.

On Project Eleven, this meant five prototype versions across two weeks — each one responding to actual feedback, not hypothetical notes. It meant that when leadership responded to the lo-fi presentation with "we want more integration, more automation," that direction became a working hi-fi prototype within hours, not a new sprint. The feedback cycle that normally takes weeks collapsed into a single session.

The key constraint worth naming: this only worked because the thinking had already happened. JTBDs, workflow maps, user actions — all defined before prompting began. The AI was precise because the inputs were precise. Vague prompting produces vague output. What made this different was treating the AI as an execution layer for decisions already made, not a shortcut around making them.

## Four stages replacing seven tools

The prototype unified seven disconnected internal systems into a single four-stage pipeline. Each stage was designed around a distinct user goal, with automation handling the transitions between them.

<SectionLabel text="Outcome" />

## Something real, in two weeks

The lo-fi prototype was presented to C-level leadership and drove immediate alignment across tech, product, and design. The response wasn't "interesting, let's plan a follow-up" — it was specific: more integration, more automation end-to-end. That feedback became a hi-fi prototype within hours. Not a new sprint, not a new brief. Hours.

That compression is the real outcome. Not just that a prototype existed in two weeks, but that the feedback loop between leadership input and working artifact had fundamentally changed. The cycle that normally spans weeks — brief, discovery, design, review — collapsed into a single working session. That's a different relationship between design and decision-making.

## What this changes about how design works

The conventional design process has a shape: understand, define, ideate, prototype, test. Each phase has a cost in time, and that cost creates selection pressure — only the most important problems get prototyped, only the best-funded projects get to hi-fi. The rest stay in decks.

What Project Eleven demonstrated is that AI tools don't just speed up individual phases — they change the economics of the whole process. A solo designer, with the right foundation and the right prompting discipline, can produce the kind of artifact that previously required a team, a handoff process, and a multi-week sprint. That changes what gets explored, what gets shown to leadership, and what decisions get made on real evidence rather than slides.

That's the shift worth naming. Not "AI made this faster" — but "AI changed what was possible for one person working alone on a complex problem under real constraints." The method is repeatable. It requires rigour upfront and precision in prompting, but it scales to complexity in a way that conventional prototyping doesn't.

> The prototype wasn't the point. What it proved was a new shape for design work — faster, denser, more connected to the decisions that matter.
