## Mapping Food Systems end-to-end

Before Project Eleven, I led a foundational research initiative to map HelloFresh's Food Systems processes from end to end — specifically in the context of migrating the US market to international tooling. This wasn't a small exercise. It meant getting deep into a highly fragmented operational landscape across multiple markets and user types.

That work gave Project Eleven its backbone. The complexity of the pipeline — and the case for unifying it — wasn't hypothetical. It was already documented.

## The challenge — A two-week deadline with no brief

The ask arrived with almost no structure: imagine what it could look like if HelloFresh could take a recipe from creation to the weekly menu in one week — and show us something real, fast. No flows, no existing UI, no spec. Just a business need and a deadline.

The strategic backdrop made it urgent. HelloFresh's most prioritised goal at the time was scaling its global offer from 50 recipes per week to 500 — a 10x expansion that would require the entire Food Systems pipeline to operate at a fundamentally different speed. The vision we were building was one of several initiatives trying to make that possible.

Seven separate internal tools handled pieces of this process. No one had ever tried to stitch them together into a single coherent experience. I had two weeks to invent the problem, define the solution, and prototype it well enough to put in front of leadership.

> Decisions that would normally take weeks had to happen in hours. There was no time to wait for clarity — I had to manufacture it.

## Structure first, then speed

The first thing I did was build a map. Before touching any tools, I ran a rapid discovery phase: whiteboard flow charts, a workflow map, and a feature set — all defined in week one. This was itself iterative — each phase of the pipeline was owned by a different PM, so getting the user goals, JTBDs, and data flows right required 1–2 check-ins with the full group. Only once that foundation was solid could I move fast without thrashing.

<FullImage src="/images/project-eleven-workflow-map.jpg" alt="Workflow map — four-stage pipeline from Validate to Inspire, Create, and Activate" contained />

I used AI tools to compress every phase that followed.

**Week 1 — Define**

Generated JTBDs, actions, tools, and a feature set using AI prompting. Built a workflow map covering the full pipeline from consumer favorites to menu activation. Total: 372 prompts across the sprint.

**Week 2 — Design**

Prompted Magic Patterns and later v0 with the workflow map and predefined user actions. Iterated directly from Figma screenshots. Ran 5 prototype versions incorporating live feedback. No handoff process — design and prototype happened simultaneously. Total: 372 prompts across the sprint.

**Now — Iterate**

Leadership feedback turned around in hours into a new prototype version (0.2). The cycle from feedback to working UI collapsed from weeks to a single session.

## Vibe coding as a design method

This project wasn't built with traditional prototyping tools. It was built by prompting — using AI agents to generate, iterate, and refine UI at a speed that no conventional workflow could match.

## Something real, in two weeks

<StatGrid v1="5" l1="Prototype iterations shipped in two weeks" v2="7→1" l2="Legacy tools unified into a single UI" v3="372" l3="AI prompts across the full sprint" />

The lo-fi prototype was presented to C-level leadership to support the vision and drive alignment across tech, product, and design. The response was immediate — leadership wanted to see the tools even more integrated, with a higher level of automation end-to-end.

Within hours of that feedback, a hi-fi prototype was created: a fully unified interface bringing R2MA, CCM, CPS, MPS, and SPT into a single experience — with automation at its core. The cycle from leadership feedback to working hi-fi prototype collapsed into a single session.
